# Question selection

Read this for stages 1, 3, 5, 6, 7 and 12: the design inventory, the candidates and their criteria, the set-aside log, reframing, the wording audit and the final selection.

## What the data can answer

Read the sampling design, the metadata and every environmental or contextual table before judging any question. For each design feature, write what it allows and what it rules out. Check in particular:

- **The unit of replication.** Several samples from one unit do not replicate the factor that distinguishes units.
- **The reference samples.** Note where they lie and how far away, and whether they match the other samples along the main gradients.
- **Missing attributes.** List samples whose attributes were never recorded.
- **Units of measurement.** Note any that are provisional or missing.
- **Plausibility.** Check whether measured values are physically possible under the recorded conditions. A value that cannot occur in situ shows the reading was taken later, and it supports no interpretation.
- **Inseparable drivers.** Check whether a putative driver co-varies so closely with a design gradient that the two cannot be separated.
- **Material and new data.** Record what material is retained and which new data are still expected.

Judge plausibility with numbers, not impressions. Ask the author for missing facts, and record the answers with their dates.

End with one sentence on the kind of question the data can carry. For example: composition data can say who is present and in what proportion, but can only raise questions about mechanism.

## Candidates and criteria

Draw candidates from five sources:

- the open elements of the first literature audit;
- what is unusual in the design;
- each new result as it appears;
- inverted failures: a result that undermines one framing can become half of another;
- original but fragile leads, which can be kept as stated hypotheses rather than claims.

| Criterion | Question asked |
| --- | --- |
| Answerable | Can the design answer it directly, without data the project lacks? |
| Robust | Does it survive restricted permutations, leaving out a unit, tests inside units and multiple-test correction? |
| Open | Does it remain unanswered after checking the closest published and grey literature? |
| General | Would the answer matter beyond the site or system studied? |
| Distinctive | Does it use what is unusual in the design, such as a rare habitat, an unusual reference, a long time series or several data types from the same samples? |

Add the wording rule: every assumption carried by the proposed wording must also be tested.

Rate each criterion Yes, Partly, No or Not run. Robust ratings must come from tests that were run, and Open ratings from audits that were logged, not from expectation.

Start an evolution log at once, with the date, the leading question and its outcome. Add a row whenever the leading question changes.

## Set-aside log

Log every candidate that fails, under one of three reasons:

- covered by other work;
- undermined by the project's own tests;
- dependent on data the project does not have.

For each, record what was tried, the decisive numbers, and why it was set aside. Also record where its remnant now sits in the paper: a supporting result, a figure, a table, a caveat or a single sentence.

Nothing is dropped silently, and a set-aside result is never quietly reused as a claim.

## Reframing

After each failure, and before proposing anything new, answer four questions in the working notes:

1. What survived, exactly, and under which tests?
2. Can a weak or null result become half of a contrast?
3. Which original but fragile leads can be stated as hypotheses, and what would test them?
4. Does the proposed title or lead sentence follow the evidence word by word?

Present each reframing to the author with its evidence; the author adopts it.

The question may take its final form only after the data have been seen. In that case, present the central result as exploratory and tested for robustness, not as a prediction confirmed on independent samples, and remove wording that implies an advance expectation.

## Wording audit

List each assumption that the title, the abstract claims and each Highlight carry. Test it, and record the decision.

| Wording | Assumption | Test | Typical decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| "only X" | No other group responds | List every responding group | "mainly X" if X dominates under every setting, with the scope stated |
| "X alone" | The relation is exclusive | Rank X among all alternatives after removing shared drivers | Reword if others do as well |
| "led by X" | X carries most of the response | Share of the response carried by X | Drop if X is a minority |
| "whole community" | Most of the community responds, in every group | Share responding, by group and with dominant groups removed | Qualify the weaker group |
| A process adjective, such as "active", "toxic" or "nitrogen-fixing" | The process or condition was measured | Was it measured, or inferred from taxonomy or a proxy? | Drop the adjective when it is only inferred |
| "n-fold" | The size does not depend on the method | Compare with a measure that selects no features | Tie the number to its measure and report both |
| "general", "consistent" or "independent of site" | The pattern does not rest on one unit | Leave each unit out | Withdraw if the pattern is lost |
| "we expected" | A prediction made in advance | When did the question take its form? | Reword if the question came after the data |

A singular article, as in "a" site or "a" system, is a limit rather than an assumption. Keep it when the claim rests on one site, because it states the claim's scope honestly.

Choose between supported wordings yourself, and record why.

## Selection

Rate every candidate on the five criteria in one matrix, and select the candidate rated Yes on both Robust and Open.

When several qualify, prefer the one that:

- asks what the data measure directly;
- uses the distinctive features of the design;
- avoids the weakest measurements;
- gives the fragile leads a logical place as hypotheses.

Give the reasons in prose, and derive the title wording from the wording audit.
